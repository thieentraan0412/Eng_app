The story of silk

The history of the world’s most luxurious fabric, from ancient China to the present day.

Silk is a fine, smooth material produced from the cocoons — soft protective shells — that are made by mulberry silkworms (insect larvae). Legend has it that it was Lei Tzu, wife of the Yellow Emperor, ruler of China in about 3000 BC, who discovered silkworms. One account of the story goes that as she was taking a walk in her husband’s gardens, she discovered that silkworms were responsible for the destruction of several mulberry trees.

She collected a number of cocoons and sat down to have a rest. It just so happened that while she was sipping some tea, one of the cocoons that she had collected landed in the hot tea and started to unravel into a fine thread. Lei Tzu found that she could wind this thread around her fingers. Subsequently, she persuaded her husband to allow her to rear silkworms on a grove of mulberry trees. She also devised a special reel to draw the fibres from the cocoon into a single thread so that they would be strong enough to be woven into fabric.

While it is unknown just how much of this is true, it is certainly known that silk cultivation has existed in China for several millennia. Originally, silkworm farming was solely restricted to women, and it was they who were responsible for the growing, harvesting and weaving. Silk quickly grew into a symbol of status, and originally, only royalty were entitled to have clothes made of silk.

The rules were gradually relaxed over the years until finally during the Qing Dynasty (1644–1911 AD), even peasants, the lowest caste, were also entitled to wear silk. Sometime during the Han Dynasty (206 BC–220 AD), silk was so prized that it was also used as a unit of currency. Government officials were paid their salary in silk.

For a long time the method of making silk — a process now known as sericulture — was one of the most closely guarded secrets in the world. Chinese law forbade anyone to carry silkworm eggs, cocoons or mulberry seeds beyond the borders of the empire, and those caught attempting to do so faced the death penalty. Finished cloth, however, could be traded freely. It was the fabric, rather than the knowledge behind it, that travelled west.

By the second century BC, under the Han Dynasty, that trade had grown into a network of caravan routes running some 6,400 kilometres from the Chinese capital to the shores of the Mediterranean. The network was given its familiar name — the Silk Road — only in 1877, by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, long after the routes themselves had fallen out of use. Silk was never the only cargo: paper, spices, glassware, horses, religions and, less welcome, epidemic disease all moved along the same roads.

The secret could not be kept forever. Sericulture reached Korea around 200 BC with Chinese settlers and spread from there to Japan, while India developed an industry of its own in the early centuries AD. The best-known account of its arrival in Europe comes from the Byzantine historian Procopius, who wrote that in about AD 550 two monks appeared before the Emperor Justinian carrying silkworm eggs hidden inside hollow bamboo canes, smuggled out of China. Whether or not the tale is accurate, Byzantium was weaving its own silk within a generation.

The process itself has changed remarkably little. A silkworm spins its cocoon from a single continuous filament that may run to between 600 and 900 metres. To keep that filament unbroken the cocoons are heated before the moth can emerge and cut its way out; they are then softened in hot water and several filaments are reeled together into one thread strong enough to weave. It takes roughly 2,500 silkworms, and the leaves of a small grove of mulberry trees, to produce a single pound of raw silk.

Today China and India account for the overwhelming majority of world production, yet silk remains a tiny fraction of the global textile market — well under one per cent of all fibre by weight. Its value has always lain elsewhere. Silk has been used for parachutes, bicycle tyres and surgical sutures, and laboratories now grow it into scaffolds for repairing human tissue. Five thousand years after Lei Tzu is said to have dropped a cocoon into her tea, the thread has not run out.
